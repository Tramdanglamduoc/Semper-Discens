Alzheimer Disease
- Part 1

It is our brain that makes us who we are: how we react in different situations, who we love, what we fear, what we miss. These fundamental aspects of human nature are vulnerable when our brains malfunction in a neurodegenerative disorder known as Alzheimer's Disease. Alzheimer's disease is a form of dementia - a global disease that affects about 5% of people 65 years old and 25% of people 85 years of age and older. This is a heartbreaking illness: it often begins with memory loss, progresses to loss of personality, and eventually death. Watching loved ones lose themselves in this way is a particularly difficult experience for a loved one. Eventually, people who are sick may not be able to recognize those closest to them and will require help with daily activities such as dressing, eating, bathing, and going to the bathroom.

What is Alzheimer disease?

Alzheimer's is an irreversible, progressive brain disorder that gradually destroys memory and cognitive skills, and eventually the ability to perform simple tasks. Although the specific cause of Alzheimer's disease is not fully known, it is characterized by changes in the brain — including amyloid plaques, or neurofibrillary tangles — that lead to the loss of nerve cells, and the connections between them. These changes affect a person's ability to remember and think.

The earliest symptoms of the disease include forgetfulness; disorientation involving time or places; and difficulty with concentration, computation, language, and judgment. In the final stages, the patient loses the ability to care for himself and may be bedridden.

It is becoming increasingly clear that Alzheimer's is a multifunctional and multifactorial disease that may involve different pathogenesis, yet the clinical outcomes are similar. Many factors can cause or affect Alzheimer's.
