Alzheimer Disease -
Part 3

It is our brain that makes us who we are: how we react in different situations, who we love, what we fear, what we miss. These fundamental aspects of human nature are vulnerable when our brains malfunction in a neurodegenerative disorder known as Alzheimer's Disease. Alzheimer's disease is a form of dementia - a global disease that affects about 5% of people 65 years old and 25% of people 85 years of age and older.

This is a heartbreaking illness: it often begins with memory loss, progresses to loss of personality, and eventually death. Watching loved ones lose themselves in this way is a particularly difficult experience for a loved one. Eventually, people who are sick may not be able to recognize those closest to them and will require help with daily activities such as dressing, eating, bathing, and going to the bathroom.

Treatments being used for Alzheimer’s disease

The FDA has approved five prescription drugs to treat Alzheimer's disease. While they relieve some symptoms, they do not cure or prevent the disease:

  • Three of these drugs are cholinesterase inhibitors: donepezil, galantamine, and rivastigmine. Cholinesterase inhibitors block the action of acetylcholinesterase, an enzyme that breaks down the neurotransmitter acetylcholine.

    -> This increases the available amount of acetylcholine (which is involved in learning and memory), counteracting the harmful effects of disease on the production of this neurotransmitter.
  • The fourth drug, memantine, is an NMDA receptor antagonist. Normally, NMDA receptors bind with the neurotransmitter glutamate, allowing calcium ions to enter neurons. In Alzheimer's disease, damaged cells become flooded with calcium ions, further damaging nerve cells — a condition known as neurotoxicity. Memantine blocks the flow of calcium ions through NMDA receptor channels.
  • Fifth drug approved is manufactured combination of memantine and donepezil. Donepezil can be used for all stages of the disease, galantamine for mild to moderate episodes, memantine for moderate to severe episodes, and rivastigmine for all stages. The donepezil/memantine cocktail is used to treat moderate to severe Alzheimer's disease.

But recently on June 7, 2021, the FDA approved a new drug called Aducanumab. Aducanumab is the only direct treatment drug currently approved for Alzheimer's disease:

  • This drug is a human antibody, or immunotherapy, that targets the beta-amyloid protein and helps reduce amyloid plaques, which are brain damage associated with Alzheimer's disease.
  • Aducanumab works to remove amyloid plaques and results in a 40% reduction in impairment in daily activities.
  • A side effect of this drug may be increased levels of inflammation in the nervous system.
  • Through research, people with the ApoE4 gene not only have a higher risk of disease but also have a high risk of strong reactions to drug side effects, so they are often treated with a relative low dose, so the effect gained is also to a certain degree.
  • Moreover, Aducanumab has been shown to be effective and is currently only being treated for people with early-onset Alzheimer's.

One drug currently in clinical trials is Lecanemab (BAN2401).

This medication also uses the same mechanism as Aducanumab.

One that has also shown promise is
the drug GV-971:

  • Research in China shows evidence of a gut-brain connection and shows a correlation between certain types of bacteria in the gut and levels of inflammation, the number of Beta Amyloid plaques, and the number of tangles in the nervous system.
  • Research suggests that the drug GV-971, by stabilizing those bacterial communities, it makes the level of inflammation, the number of Beta Amyloid plaques, the number of neurofibrillary tangles in the nervous system will decrease.
  • This drug is currently being sold on the Chinese market as of November 2019.

Another treatment strategy, based on the amyloid hypothesis, uses the body's immune response to attack and clear the amyloid plaques:

  • Trials of active immunity (training the immune system to build a person's antibodies) and passive immunization (infusion of active defense antibodies without fortifying one's own immune system)) have all been discovered.
  • To date, however, these types of therapies have not been suitable for people with moderate to severe symptoms.

Experimented on mice, Lithium has been shown to reduce tau phosphorylation:

  • Lithium treatment has been shown to reduce the density of nerve disturbances in transgenic models in the hippocampus and spinal cord.
  • Although the density of NFTs decreased, memory loss did not improve after treatment. Lithium also did not have a preventive effect on Alzheimer's.
